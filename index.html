Pushing humanity's frontier into space is the problem I want to spend my life on. The technology is the easy half.

To quote Thomas Zurbuchen, who was NASA's head of science for six years and now runs ETH Zurich Space:

“The hardest system to manage in NASA missions is not the science system, or the engineering system. It's the people system.”

That has matched everything I've seen. My journey through the technology side has taken me from rocket engines and cryogenic valves at ARIS, ETH Zürich's rocketry team, to then leading technical advisory for the next generation rocket at ARIS. Later, at NASA JPL I worked on signal processing on a radio telescope meant to sit inside a crater on the far side of the Moon. Right now I'm at MIT, writing my master's thesis on satellite trajectory optimisation and space weather modelling. Propulsion, robotics, controls, simulation — I have yet to meet an aerospace problem I wasn't interested in.

But the other half of the system is people, and it can be the harder one. Nine years of teaching, tutoring and mentoring, from school maths to first-year ETH students. Co-founding POLANA, now the largest Polish student association in Switzerland. Chairing European Youth Parliament sessions of a hundred delegates who all arrived certain they were right. I spend my time on this because I enjoy it, and because every project I've watched stall did so mainly because of organisation or communication, rather than equations and engineering.

Rocketry & space

05

Visiting Student Researcher in the Robotics Group at JPL, working on the Lunar Crater Radio Telescope — a 350m diameter telescope to be placed in a crater on the moon. The goal of this telescope is to measure the Dark Ages signal - an as yet unmeasured signal from the Universe's infancy.

Built the signal processing pipeline, an orbital simulations toolkit, and an antenna/foreground convolved signal extraction pipeline using machine learning and analytical methods such as convolved singular value decomposition. The main challenge is separating the signal we indend to measure, which has a strength of 10^-3 Kelvin, from the noisy foreground such as galaxies, which can have a strenght of up to 10^5 Kelvin. This discrepancy in orders of magnitude discrepancy is like trying to find one specific sand grain on the beach, with the wind blowing.

I built a test stand for measuring micropump performance at pd|z, ETH's product development group — and had to design and build the pump first, because there wasn't one to build the stand around.

The pump went through four generations of casing before you could even get the impeller back out of it. It runs at 10,000 rpm, and the stand reads inlet and outlet pressure and mass flow while you throttle the outlet — which is what draws the head-against-flow curve that tells you whether an impeller is any good.

My favourite part was working out why the shaft seal kept destroying itself: a quarter of a millimetre of misalignment. Corrected that and it ran for hours. The printed casing eventually started spraying water through its own walls, which is a fairly persuasive argument for machining the next one.

Propellant supply engineer on a liquid rocket engine test stand, and lead on the cryogenic valve system.

ARIS is the the Academic Swiss Spaceflight Initiative, and HELIOS was our second generation bi-liquid rocket engine test stand. The ultimate goal of student rocketry is to launch a bi-liquid rocket, and project HELIOS was a stepping stone towards this.

Building, developing and operating the rocket engine was honestly some of the most fun I have ever had while engineering. Sure, it was terrifying when one wrong move meant that liquid oxygen at -184°C will explode in your face, but it was incredibly rewarding when the engine finally fired. I was responsible for propellant supply, which means designing and maintaining the fuel and oxidiser supply lines. In the end the engine delivered 665 N of thrust at 26.7 bar chamber pressure, and held a twenty-second burn.

Cryogenic ball valves on rocket engines freeze shut, jam, and refuse to throttle. They are also bulky and slow to cool, and between them those faults have ended more than one test campaign. My thesis was two upgrades to that valve, both only possible if you print it.

The first morphs the port from a circle into the V and back, instead of cutting a V into a sphere. Smoothing the transition alone changed nothing, but opening the port out to match the pipe's area took the pressure drop across a fully open valve from 0.9 kPa to zero.

The second runs a cooling channel through the valve walls, filled with a printed lattice. Thirty seconds into pre-cooling, a standard valve has a 96°C gradient across the ball. Mine has three, and the final design is 18.5% lighter.

This is also where I properly learned CFD — meshing, turbulence models, boundary conditions, and how much of a simulation's credibility rests on validating it against something you already know the answer to.

Robotics & software

04

Writing my master's thesis at the MIT Star Lab in collaboration with ETH Space Geodesy. The focus of the research is to optimise satellite trajectory control and maneuvering using a space-weather aware machine learning models. Yearly we lose dozens of satellites due to solar activity.

Solving this means first developing a machine learning prediction model for space weather phenomena, such as iono and thermospheric coupling effects following solar storms, and taking that data to compute orbital parameters such as drag. This is to be then plugged into the satellite trajectory controllers, and the goal is to reduce satellite loss and improve control.

A research project at ETH on whether a drone swarm can arrange itself to fly in its own wake. A drone drags a cone of downwash behind it that costs anything following it energy, and two cones of upwash either side that give it back for free.

The point of the project is that the control is distributed. There is no central computer and no drone that knows where everyone else is — each one sees only its neighbours and decides for itself, which is the only version that scales past a handful of drones and the only version that survives losing one mid-flight.

We ran our own CFD to pin down the shape of the wake, modelled it as a field with Gaussian falloff, and built three controllers against it: a linear case simple enough to solve on paper, a gradient-based one, and distributed model predictive control.

All three converge. The gradient controller runs in 0.01 seconds an iteration and is light enough to fly on board; MPC finds the better formation almost every time, at 2.1 seconds. I came out of it having properly learned distributed control theory — how you prove a set of independent agents will settle at all.

Leadership & teaching

03

Co-founded the Polish Academic Network Abroad and run external relations — outreach, partner acquisition, sponsors, and events for up to 100 people!

Started as a handful of us who, upon realising that students from other countries have their own clubs, while the Polish students didn't, decided to act on this. When I started studying in Zurich, there was no community, and it was difficult to get a foothold, to find friends and to get started studying. We decided to change this. What started as a group of 10 friends has since grown to 200+ member organisation!

We have hosted and organised events such as traditional dinners, Christmas Eve, as well as sporting and recreational events, and also academic events. All with the intention of creating and fostering a community, helping people make lasting friendships, and promoting Polish culture abroad.

The European Youth Parliament runs conferences across the continent where a few hundred people spend a week writing and defending resolutions on whatever Europe is currently arguing about. The point of it is to get young people genuinely engaged in sociopolitical and economic questions rather than watching them from a distance.

The largest I have presided over was Trento in 2026 — 100+ delegates, eight committees — and Sassari before it, also over a hundred. I have chaired committees at sessions across the continent since 2018, among them Germany and Ghent in Belgium.

The connections and the learning that come out of a week like that are hard to overstate. Very little else teaches leadership, creativity and confidence at that rate.
