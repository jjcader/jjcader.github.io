Pushing humanity's frontier into space is the problem I want to spend my life on. The technology is the easy half.

To quote Thomas Zurbuchen, who was NASA's head of science for six years and now runs ETH Zurich Space:

“The hardest system to manage in NASA missions is not the science system, or the engineering system. It's the people system.”

That has matched everything I've seen. My journey through the technology side has taken me from rocket engines and cryogenic valves at ARIS, ETH Zürich's rocketry team, to then leading technical advisory for the next generation rocket at ARIS. Later, at NASA JPL I worked on signal processing on a radio telescope meant to sit inside a crater on the far side of the Moon. Right now I'm at MIT, writing my master's thesis on satellite trajectory optimisation and space weather modelling. Propulsion, robotics, controls, simulation — I have yet to meet an aerospace problem I wasn't interested in.

But the other half of the system is people, and it can be the harder one. Nine years of teaching, tutoring and mentoring, from school maths to first-year ETH students. Co-founding POLANA, now the largest Polish student association in Switzerland. Chairing European Youth Parliament sessions of a hundred delegates who all arrived certain they were right. I spend my time on this because I enjoy it, and because every project I've watched stall did so mainly because of organisation or communication, rather than equations and engineering.

Rocketry & space

05

Visiting Student Researcher in the Robotics Group at JPL, working on the Lunar Crater Radio Telescope — a 350m diameter telescope to be placed in a crater on the moon. The goal of this telescope is to measure the Dark Ages signal - an as yet unmeasured signal from the Universe's infancy.

Built the signal processing pipeline, an orbital simulations toolkit, and an antenna/foreground convolved signal extraction pipeline using machine learning and analytical methods such as convolved singular value decomposition. The main challenge is separating the signal we indend to measure, which has a strength of 10^-3 Kelvin, from the noisy foreground such as galaxies, which can have a strenght of up to 10^5 Kelvin. This discrepancy in orders of magnitude discrepancy is like trying to find one specific sand grain on the beach, with the wind blowing.

Built a test stand for measuring micropump performance at pd|z, ETH's product development group, and had to design and build the pump first, because there wasn't one to build the stand around.

The pump went through four generations of casing, all FDM-printed in PLA, and standardising the assembly on M8 screws took it apart and back together in five minutes instead of an hour. It measures inlet and outlet pressure, mass flow and speed to ISO 9906.

Two things nearly killed it. The shaft lip seal ground its way into the printed shaft and destroyed itself — 0.25mm of misalignment. And PLA stays water-permeable above 40% infill, so past a certain pressure the pump stopped leaking at the joints and started spraying through its own walls. It reached 10 L/min and 0.4 bar; the next casing should be metal.

Two changes to a cryogenic ball valve, both only possible if you print it. The brief came from ARIS's PROMETHEUS test stand, where the valves jammed, froze and couldn't throttle the engine.

First, morph the port from a circle into the V and back, instead of cutting a V into a sphere. Smoothing the transition alone changed nothing; opening the port out to match the pipe's area took the pressure drop across a fully open valve from 0.9 kPa to zero.

Second, a cooling channel through the valve walls, filled with a printed lattice. Thirty seconds into pre-cooling, a standard valve has a 96°C gradient across the ball; the jacketed one has three. The final design is 18.5% lighter.

The hardest number in the thesis was the viscosity of liquid oxygen. The best source is from 1971.

Four of us at ETH, on whether a drone swarm can arrange itself to fly in its own wake. A drone drags a cone of downwash behind it, which costs anything following it energy, and two cones of upwash either side, which give it back for free.

We ran our own CFD to pin the shape down, modelled it as a field with Gaussian falloff, then built three distributed controllers — no central computer, each drone using only its neighbours' positions.

The comparison is the result. Gradient-based control runs in 0.01 seconds an iteration and would fit on the drone, but settles into local maxima. Model predictive control finds the right formation almost every time, at 2.1 seconds an iteration — two hundred times too slow to fly.
