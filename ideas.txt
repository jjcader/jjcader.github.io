BIG IDEAS
================================================================================

1 — MISSION LOG
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A dated, append-only log. Two sentences each, mono, timestamped.

    T+ 2026.03.14   Third valve seat iteration. Leaks at 40 bar, holds at 35.
                    The problem is the seal geometry, not the material.

A blog promises articles and an abandoned one looks like failure. A log
promises nothing, so a three-month gap just means nothing happened. It is the
only thing that would show HOW you think rather than what you finished — which
is what a research group is actually trying to find out.
Own page, linked from the footer. One file you append to.


2 — ANNOTATED HARDWARE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
One full-bleed photo of something you built. Numbered hotspots. Click one, get
a note: what the part does, what went wrong, what you'd change.

    (2) Propellant line. Routed three times before this one survived a test.
    (3) This bracket is the only part that never failed. I have no idea why.

A photo with a caption says "I was there". A photo you can take apart says "I
know what every part does and I remember how each one failed" — a different
claim, and hard to fake. Turns the site's weakest asset into its strongest.
Between Highlights and Everything. One object, done properly.
Needs one genuinely good, well-lit photograph. Worth taking deliberately.


3 — THE FAILURE INDEX
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A short section listing things that did not work. Ranked by how expensive the
lesson was.

    01   Printed a valve seat in the wrong material. Held pressure for nine
         seconds. Cost: three weeks and a test slot.
    02   Assumed the swarm controller would scale from four drones to twelve.
         It did not. Cost: an entire semester's assumption.
    03   Chaired a session without a timekeeper. Cost: two committees never
         finished drafting.

WHY THIS IS THE OUT-OF-THE-BOX ONE
Every portfolio on earth lists successes. This is the only page anyone would
actually remember, and it inverts the format completely — the content that
every other candidate is actively hiding becomes the thing you lead with.

It works specifically for you because your field makes it credible: in
rocketry, hardware failing is the normal case, and an engineer who can name
their own failure modes precisely is demonstrably more competent than one who
can't. Publishing it is only possible if you genuinely understand what went
wrong, which is exactly the signal you want to send. The bragging is implicit
and much stronger than a list of achievements.

It also solves a real problem this site has: everything currently reads as
polished and finished, so there is nothing to distinguish "I was on this
project" from "I ran into this wall and got past it".

Risk: tone. It has to read as confident, not self-deprecating — the format is
an engineering post-mortem, not an apology. Keep the entries short, factual,
and always name the cost. Never explain the lesson; let the reader draw it.
Five entries maximum. Six would make it a theme.
